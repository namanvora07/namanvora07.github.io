Multivariant Transmission Assembly Line Design

01
6-, 8- and 9-speed transmission line · 3-scenario throughput & cost optimization · Clemson University
Siemens Plant Simulation Line Balancing Cost Modeling MS Excel
CHALLENGE

Stand up a production floor for a new transmission, capable of assembling three variants — 6-, 8-, and 9-speed — to meet 70,000 units/year. The line had to hit that target under real conditions: machine failures, cycle-time variability, personnel breaks, and finite floor space. Two risks dominated: starvation cost from an unbalanced line, and cross-variant mismatching during assembly.

APPROACH
  • Built the model in three escalating scenarios — Ideal (no failures or variability), Typical (MTTF/MTTR, resources, variability, in-line buffers), and Real (minimum cost under a hard 2-shift, 5-day constraint).
  • Ran machine-selection cost analysis in Excel, finding that combining Processes A and B on one machine and Processes C and D on another beat separate stations on both cost and waiting time.
  • Added convertors on the bell-housing and bearing-housing conveyors that read each part's variant processing time and routed it to the matching assembly station, eliminating cross-variant mismatches.
  • Identified starvation as over 90% of total operating cost, then attacked it directly — upgrading to Okuma M3453 machines (6% vs. 15% failure rate), adding a seventh machine on the bottleneck process, and eliminating the vertical buffer store in favor of just-in-time flow.
  • Modeled worker footpaths and staircases so personnel could reach stations without crossing robot or conveyor hazard zones.
OUTCOME

Cut line waiting time from 62.84% to 43.96% and reduced total annual operating cost by 53.3% — from $642.8M to $300.4M — while dropping from three shifts to two and from 36 workers to 30. The final layout produced 73,434 units/year: 104.9% of target, a safe buffer for quality fallout without the 19.8% overproduction and inventory burden of the typical-case layout. A $316K investment in more reliable machinery returned $337.7M in avoided starvation cost.

Brush Assembly Plant Layout Optimization

02
Morgan Advanced Materials, Greenville SC · Lean Six Sigma Green Belt capstone · Clemson University
Six Sigma DMAIC Siemens Plant Simulation AutoCAD Spaghetti Analysis Process Mapping
CHALLENGE

Morgan Advanced Materials' Industrial Low Volume carbon-brush area had grown without a layout strategy. Equipment from other production areas sat in the footprint, machines were placed without flow logic, and parts travelled highly non-linear paths across machining, thermal processing, and assembly. The result was excessive worker motion, wasted floor space, and a workflow too tangled to supervise effectively.

APPROACH
  • Scoped the project on industry-advisor guidance to the top 8 parts by annual volume — 40,322 units — rather than the full 40,000-part catalogue.
  • Mapped the full process from drill/score/dedust through auto-bagging into five swimlane categories, then built baseline spaghetti charts and measured worker travel distance per part with a measuring wheel.
  • Ran a 5-Why root-cause analysis, tracing excessive movement back to a layout that had evolved without any standardized, process-based redesign.
  • Divided the floor into six sequential process zones — machining, product treatment, pad station, electrical assembly, finishing, packing — to force straight-line flow and eliminate backtracking.
  • Iterated the layout in AutoCAD and validated flow and throughput in Siemens Plant Simulation, correcting a first iteration that was too cluttered and under-spaced.
OUTCOME

Cut average worker travel from 278.6 to 96.5 ft per part — a 65.36% reduction in motion waste — and shrank the floor footprint from 8,844 to 3,724 sq ft, freeing 57.89% of the area for additional lines. Daily worker travel dropped from roughly 8.6 miles to 3 miles, recovering 2.06 hours of productive time per day and an estimated $12,757 in annual labor cost. Queue racks and inclined conveyors were recommended as the next phase.
