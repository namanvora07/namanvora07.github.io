Series–Parallel Hybrid Powertrain Modeling & Control

01
Stateflow supervisory control · battery electro-thermal modeling · measured drive-cycle validation · Clemson University
MATLAB/Simulink Stateflow Battery Modeling BSFC Mapping GPS Drive Cycle
CHALLENGE

Size and compare conventional, battery-electric, and hybrid powertrains for a family sedan, then design a hybrid that actually beats both. The hard part isn't the hardware — it's the supervisory logic: deciding moment to moment whether to run on motor, engine, or both, while keeping the engine near its BSFC sweet spot, the battery inside safe state-of-charge bounds, and the pack under thermal limits.

APPROACH
  • Collected a real 37.8-mile drive cycle by GPS logger (CU-ICAR to Clemson main campus) and derived tractive power demand — 52 kW peak, 40.4 kWh at the wheels — as the sizing basis.
  • Modeled all three architectures in Simulink with a forward-facing, closed-loop structure: PID driver model, supervisory control layer, BSFC-based gear-shift map, drivetrain torque flow, and vehicle dynamics.
  • Downsized the engine to a 1.5 L, 57 kW unit and paired it with an HPEVS AC-23 motor, sizing a 20s35p pack from the same cells used in the BEV study.
  • Built a Stateflow supervisory controller with five state variables (engine, motor, clutch, regen, charging) across Economy and Sport modes, with SoC limits adjusted dynamically against vehicle speed — faster vehicles can afford lower reserves because regen will replenish them.
  • Modeled battery SoC, OCV, internal resistance, heat generation, and PID-controlled coolant flow, and unit-tested the control logic against forced input combinations before running the full cycle.
OUTCOME

The hybrid achieved 64 MPG against 43 MPG for the conventional powertrain — a 21 MPG gain — with 90 kW combined output and 819 miles of range on a 12.8-gallon tank. Pack temperature held below 32 °C throughout the cycle under closed-loop coolant control. A full cost comparison across all three architectures, accounting for purchase price, fuel/charging, and battery replacement, recommended the series-parallel hybrid as the best balance of performance, range, and roughly 49% fuel-cost savings.

Nonlinear Vehicle Stability Control

02
Feedback linearization vs. sliding mode yaw control · 7-DOF plant · CarSim validation · Clemson University
MATLAB/Simulink CarSim Sliding Mode Control Feedback Linearization FMVSS 126
CHALLENGE

Standard stability controllers are built on linearized models like the 2-DOF bicycle model, which hold up in normal driving but break down exactly where drivers most need help — outside the linear region of tire cornering stiffness. Drivers apply the same linear mental model, overcorrect, and induce pendulum behavior ending in spin-out. The task was to design nonlinear controllers that stay valid in that regime, and to determine which approach is actually viable in a real vehicle.

APPROACH
  • Built a 7-DOF double-track nonlinear plant model with state-dependent tire cornering stiffness, using pure-slip lateral force data for a 215/55/R17 tire extracted from CarSim.
  • Derived a Feedback Linearization control law that algebraically cancels the vehicle's natural yaw dynamics, using a linearized two-track nominal model with cornering stiffness varying across eight normal-load intervals.
  • Derived a Sliding Mode control law that treats the same cancellation as equivalent control, then adds a discontinuous switching term to drive error to zero regardless of unmodeled dynamics — replacing the sign function with a saturation boundary layer to suppress actuator chattering.
  • Tested both under FMVSS 126 Sine-with-Dwell at 80 km/h and ISO 3888-1 Double Lane Change at 60 and 120 km/h, with reference yaw rate saturated to physically attainable limits.
  • Cross-validated controller performance against a high-fidelity CarSim plant with combined-slip tire dynamics.
OUTCOME

Both controllers tracked reference yaw rate through aggressive steering inputs, but SMC proved the stronger option: it held smaller deviation from target at a gain of 100 s⁻¹, where FBL overshot even at 300 s⁻¹. The saturation function eliminated high-frequency chattering, producing a smooth corrective moment curve — meaningful for actuator durability and passenger comfort, not just plot aesthetics. Control effort at peak comprised roughly 62.5% of total vehicle yaw moment. FBL was found to lack the robustness real automotive applications demand; SMC delivered equivalent tracking at lower control expenditure, with Super-Twisting SMC identified as the next step.
